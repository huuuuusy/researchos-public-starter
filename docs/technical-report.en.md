# ReSearchOS: A Reference Architecture for Sustained Human–AI Research

Shiyu Hu[^author-note] · Technical report · 30 October 2026 · Not peer reviewed

School of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore

[shiyu.hu@ntu.edu.sg](mailto:shiyu.hu@ntu.edu.sg)

> As execution becomes easier to delegate, researchers must decide what is worth asking, what is sufficient grounds for judgment, and what is worth retaining.

## Agent Summary

In human–AI research that runs for weeks or months, later work builds on earlier judgments. Yet unless they are recorded, the reasons for those judgments and the conditions under which they apply tend to be lost when a conversation ends, and the sources they rest on can change. Suppose a paper the researcher relied on is corrected weeks later. The existing judgments can neither all stand nor all be discarded: (1) what the correction leaves intact should be kept with its grounds; (2) only the part it touches needs updating; and (3) what the evidence cannot yet settle stays unresolved until the researcher answers. Telling these apart takes an understanding of the evidence and cannot be recomputed automatically as in a build system, so the decision belongs to the researcher, while the system finds the judgments recorded as resting on that source and hands them back. On this basis the report presents ReSearchOS, a research environment owned by the researcher and kept mainly in local files. It keeps judgments with their grounds and scope, and an AI model's output remains a candidate until the researcher admits it. The report gives the design principles, definitions that make the three operations checkable and a reference architecture, together with a command-line prototype and a downloadable version to try. At its next command the prototype notices a changed source, registers a recheck of the related judgments and refuses a candidate whose source changed during review; by default the downloadable version only collects candidates and does not change the researcher's records. The prototype implements part of the design, and its effect awaits a study of use and a comparison with simpler approaches.

**Keywords:** human–AI collaboration; research-state maintenance; reference architecture; judgment continuity; extensibility.

## 1. Research state, not research files

In human–AI research that runs for weeks or months, a researcher works across many sessions, sometimes with several people, and later work builds on earlier judgments. The sources behind those judgments change over time, so getting the current step right is not enough: what is carried forward also has to be checked again.

The information that check needs is easily lost. Unless they are recorded, the reasons for a judgment tend to disappear when a conversation ends. The files survive, but they do not say how a conclusion was reached, under which conditions it applies or what remains unresolved. Suppose a researcher reads a paper, concludes that method $A$ is better than method $B$ and writes down “use $A$”. Weeks later the paper is corrected, and no record shows which conclusions rest on it. The same week the researcher's student asks an AI assistant in another session to read the corrected paper, and the assistant concludes that $A$ will also be better in a situation the paper never tested. Whoever continues from that one line has nothing else to go on.

Fuller records help, but they cannot settle what should follow a change, because a judgment is not a function of its sources. In a build system an output is a function of its inputs: when an input changes, what depends on it is rebuilt and no one is asked (Figure 1a). One correction, by contrast, can leave part of a judgment standing, make another part need revision and leave a third undecidable, and telling them apart takes someone who understands the evidence (Figure 1b).

A system can therefore find the judgments recorded as resting on a changed source and hand them back, while the decision stays with the researcher. For the same reason, what an AI model or another session produces remains a candidate until the researcher admits it (Figure 1c); neither they nor the files should make these choices for the researcher. What is admitted is a judgment, not a file, and it has to carry its grounds and limits, so what has to be kept is research state: those judgments and what is still unresolved, in files the researcher owns.

The example shows what such a decision involves. The paper ran two benchmark tests and found $A$ better on both, but it tested only one kind of situation and says nothing about whether $A$ stays ahead in another; that question is left for an experiment the researcher plans to run later. The correction finds the numbers in the second test wrong, and with the right numbers $A$'s advantage on that test disappears.

The first test was not wrong, so its support for the conclusion still stands and is **kept**. The second test changed, so the part of the conclusion that rested on it is **updated**, and nothing else is touched. The untested situation still has no answer, so it is put on **hold**: it stays open for the planned experiment. The assistant's claim, which nothing in the paper supports, is refused. Keep, update and hold all ask the same question: what does the new evidence reach?

From here on the conclusion is called $J$. The two methods are visual tracking methods, which follow a target through a video. The paper tested them only while the target stays in view, so in full $J$ reads “$A$ is better than $B$ while the target stays in view”. The untested situation is the one in which the target leaves the view and comes back. The report calls these two situations the in-view condition and the re-appearance condition. Figure 1 draws the example in the notation used throughout the report.

Squares: sources; diamonds: judgments; dashed: candidates; blue, green, red-orange: keep, update, hold.](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-contrast-en.png)

What has to survive such a change is research state: the question, the evidence and its relations, the accepted judgments with their scope, the open conditions and the supported next step. A note saying only “use $A$” loses all of this, down to the open condition and the experiment that comes next. This is the report's answer: what has to be kept is research state, not research files, and when a source changes, the researcher, not the AI model or another session, decides which part is kept, updated or left open.[^lineage]

Research state changes in three ways, and the example has all three. Time passes: the paper is corrected, sessions end, and tools and AI models may be replaced. Conditions change when the purpose turns to re-appearance. Participants change when another session proposes and someone must decide what counts as correct. The tools researchers already use can keep and update records, and some flag a changed source, or a claim that rests on it, to a person. What none of the classes compared in Section 6.1 does is keep a judgment with its scope and basis version and hand only the part a change touches back to the researcher to decide; that section runs the same correction through each of them.

ReSearchOS is a researcher-owned, local-primary environment built for the moment a source changes. Its single rule of authority is the one reached above: machine outputs begin as candidates, that is, outputs not yet admitted, and only the researcher admits a candidate into research state. Admission, the step that turns a candidate into research state, needs that decision and keeps a receipt, the record of what was done and checked (Section 4.1).

The name **ReSearchOS** comes from Re Search: returning to material with a new question and retaining what that examination changes.[^name] In the environment, researchers and computational assistants form questions, examine evidence and revise judgments across sessions through shared research objects. We call the aim it serves judgment continuity: recovering the grounds of earlier work and reconsidering whether they still apply. The environment keeps the shared records and the unresolved work; the work itself happens in agent sessions, at the command line or in specialist tools, which the environment does not orchestrate.

For the researcher, the rule sits in one loop: recover the research state that matters now, advance the research in whatever client does the work, and confirm only what deserves to enter research state. Recovery selects the minimum context for the work in hand rather than the whole repository. Confirmation asks what real change the session produced that the future should inherit, and it writes nothing when there is none. Section 2 shows the loop as the six commands of one session.

### Three contributions

The first contribution, in Section 3, is a set of design principles stating what the environment must preserve so that the choice among keep, update and hold stays with the researcher: human judgment, conditional composition, records that outlast tools and correctable accumulation.

The second, in Section 4, is a set of definitions that make the principles checkable. They describe research state and what keep, update and hold each require of maintenance work, one subsection apiece (4.1–4.3). They then compose the three within bounded goals, units of maintenance work with a frozen scope and an explicit outcome (4.4).

The third, in Sections 2 and 5, is a reference architecture and prototype that take the definitions down to the commands a researcher actually runs. At its next command the prototype notices a changed source and registers a recheck of the judgments that rest on it. At the terminal it admits a narrowed judgment only on the researcher's decision, as a new judgment block beside the original; it refuses a candidate whose source changed during review and will not overwrite another's edit. A reader can download a version to try. It is copy-only by default: it collects candidates but never admits them.

**How to read this report.** Section 2 shows one session, Section 3 says why the environment is built as it is, Section 4 gives the definitions, Section 5 sets out how the prototype is built and its gaps, and Section 6 compares neighboring systems and makes the case against. A first pass covers this section, Section 2 and the principles in Table 3. A reader deciding whether to try the environment should start with Section 6.2, which makes the case for using a strong model with a one-page handoff instead; if that handoff wins, ReSearchOS should shrink to its smallest form. Appendix A lists the objects of the definitions and the lifecycle of a requirement, and Appendix B is the implementation record. Supplementary Note 5 states every definition precisely and argues the two propositions, and Note 6 lists every gap.

## 2. A session after the paper is corrected

The session follows the example of Section 1; the methods $A$ and $B$ are fictional, and no real method's performance is reported. Figure 2 draws it. Each of its six steps is one command of `ros.py`, the program that runs a session. Beforehand the researcher had bound $J$, “$A$ is better than $B$ while the target stays in view”, to the paper at version $v_1$, grounded in both benchmark sets. The unknown under the re-appearance condition was among the open conditions, and the re-appearance experiment was planned. Then the paper is corrected to $v_2$: the second set's numbers change and the first set's do not.

![A session after a source correction: a fictional example.](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-worked-example-en.png)

The correction reaches the records when the user, or an agent the user instructs, replaces the local file that records the paper, here `sources/paper.md`, and the next `ros` command sees its changed content digest. The session opens with `ros today`, which sees the version change and registers the recheck of $J$ as open work. It asks no question yet: the correction may only fix a typo and leave the judgment valid, so whether to keep or update is still undecided.

Next, `ros context` delivers the permitted sources and the current research state with a receipt, and `ros run judgment --target <card> --executor copy_packet` writes a packet for the card carrying $J$ without calling any AI service. The packet is a Markdown file carrying the task and the delivered context, which the user pastes into any assistant. `ros collect <run> --file draft.md` then reads the assistant's draft back as a candidate. Only the one to three judgment blocks in the draft are read, and anything else is ignored:

```
> [!judgment] j-first-set
> Conclusion: A is better than B while the target stays in view, on the first benchmark set; the second-set claim is withdrawn.
> Basis: sources/paper.md@<first 8 characters of the corrected version>
```

Here the basis pins the paper at $v_2$. A basis is recorded per source file, as its path with a content digest, so the prototype cannot see the split between the first and second sets inside the paper; the candidate states which part the correction touches and the researcher decides (gap 11). A basis that names no version is pinned to the version current at collection, and a draft pinned to an outdated version is refused. The candidate leaves the re-appearance condition and the experiment alone, because they have independent grounds: withdrawing part of $A$'s advantage implies neither that $B$ is better nor that the experiment should be cancelled.

The researcher then decides, and `ros admit <candidate>` performs admission. Only after the researcher's review does it write the narrowed judgment, as a new judgment block beside $J$, and because the decision was confirmed at the terminal it is recorded as the researcher's. This step is what update means in the prototype. $J$ stays on its card, still pinned to $v_1$; keep and update both land in the new block beside it, and the prototype does not edit $J$ itself.

In the same session a second candidate, “$A$ is better after re-appearance as well”, is collected from the student's AI assistant, with its basis at $v_2$. The paper reports nothing about re-appearance, so the researcher refuses it at the terminal. The refusal is recorded as the researcher's decision; nothing is written and research state does not change. The session ends with `ros close`, which compares the sources at its start and end and lists what changed, what was admitted, what was refused, which candidates are still undecided and what is still open.

Admission can also refuse on its own, in two cases, and neither refusal is a scientific judgment. If the paper changes again during review, admission rejects the candidate as stale and asks for a new proposal on the current material. If someone else edits the file carrying $J$ after the write is planned and before it lands, the content the write was planned against no longer matches, and the system refuses to overwrite that edit. An edit made during the review itself is kept, because the write is planned only after the decision, against the file as it then stands.

The downloadable version, in its default copy-only mode, runs the same commands on its own fictional fixture, passed with `--root`, so its runs are not evidence for this session. As Table 2 shows, it may write runtime records, packets and candidates locally, but it yields only copyable candidates or planned differences and never admits a candidate or changes the researcher's sources:

Set against Section 1, the session comes to three things. The researcher kept the first-set result, which carries on in the new block with its in-view scope. The researcher updated the conclusion by withdrawing the second-set claim in that same block. Hold came down to leaving things alone: the session touches neither the re-appearance condition nor the experiment, which the prototype does not register (gap 12), and the refused proposal changed neither. At `ros close` the recheck of $J$ is still listed as open.

## 3. What a research environment must preserve

Section 2 left the decision about $J$ with the researcher, and a research environment has to be designed so that it stays there. The design starts from one sentence: research is decomposed until a judgment can be made, then composed back into research state that can be continued. When research state changes, the sentence asks for exactly keep, update and hold: what still stands, what must be revised and what must wait for an answer.

Each principle guards against one way these operations go wrong, and the four follow the life of a judgment as it is made, reused, carried across tools and corrected. Where efficiency, control and retention pull against each other, the principles put four things first: consequential human judgment, conditional composition, records that outlast tools and correctable accumulation. Figure 3 traces how they lead through the definitions and the architecture down to the code.

![**Principles, definitions, architecture and implementation each answer one question, and a failure returns to the layer responsible for it.** Each layer is drawn by what it says about the running example, and gray tags give where it is in this report. The coordination policy and a task that tests it are the next step and are not in this report.](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-derivation-en.png)

The principles set no uniform directories, methods, interfaces or review arrangements. Because explanations take time to read and maintain, and review can interrupt work or harden into habit, inspection is concentrated on the transitions that matter, and routine work goes on within the scope already agreed.

The principles come from the author's own design history, and their rules are written in six internal design documents; this section summarizes those documents and has been checked against them. Table 3 gives each principle's question, commitment and mechanism. [Supplementary Note 1](technical-report-supplement.en.md#supplementary-note-1-principle-sources-and-examples) gives the examples and the sources behind each principle, and [Supplementary Note 3](technical-report-supplement.en.md#supplementary-note-3-process-and-component-contracts) restates the provisions in full with their owners.

| Correctable accumulation | What changes after correction? | Correction narrows affected claims without erasing history; failure, unknowns and unverified states remain visible | Judgment revision, rollback receipts and capability evidence accumulated across sessions |

### Only the researcher turns a candidate into state

The first failure is keeping what was never accepted. In the example, the student's AI assistant proposes that $A$ is also better after re-appearance; admitted without the researcher's decision, that proposal would widen $J$ to a case the paper gives no grounds for. The principle is therefore that only the researcher decides what becomes research state. Admission carries the principle: a candidate enters research state only when all five conditions of admission hold, the first being the researcher's decision (Section 4.1). Admission settles who accepted a candidate. Whether the candidate is right is still a matter for later checks, since agreement does not establish scientific correctness.

### A successor runs only on an established result

The second failure is updating on an unknown. In the example, success while the target stays in view may motivate the re-appearance experiment, but recommending $A$ after the target leaves would update on exactly the unknown the experiment must settle. Hence a successor starts only when the predecessor's actual result establishes its conditions. Composition carries conditions, unknowns and failures forward (Section 4.4), and a three-valued check returns pass, fail or cannot yet tell, where cannot yet tell never counts as satisfied (Section 4.3). Fields that match between two steps let information pass between them, but they show neither that it is valid nor that its use is permitted.

### Records outlast the tools that wrote them

The third failure is losing what should have been kept. If $J$ and its grounds lived only in the conversation that produced them, nothing would show that the later correction of the paper touches $J$. A record should still make sense after the conversation window closes and the plugin that produced it stops running, so readable, versionable local sources remain authoritative, while AI models, clients and plugins can be replaced. A view that shows work as complete does not make it accepted, and a cache never becomes a second source of facts.

In the prototype, $J$ sits on a card with its basis pinned to a source version; context is delivered with a receipt (Section 2), and adapters keep no second store of facts (Section 5.1). Retention is not retrieval, so a record is kept with its conditions of use, and later work can judge whether it still applies.

### Correction narrows a claim; it does not erase history

The fourth failure is updating by deletion. In the example the correction touches only the second benchmark set, yet deleting $J$ would erase both its first-set ground, which the correction leaves intact, and the record that the second-set claim was once accepted. So correction narrows the affected claims without erasing history, and failures, unknowns and unverified states stay visible. What changed, why it changed and which parts remain useful are all kept. The narrowed judgment is admitted beside $J$ (Section 2), and the recheck of $J$ leaves the unresolved set only through a recorded resolution or cancellation (Section 4.2). A single success establishes no rule, and the report assumes no benefit to the researcher's understanding.

## 4. What keep, update and hold require

The principles say what must be preserved but not yet what counts as preserving it. This section gives definitions that make them checkable: it states keep, update and hold in words on the example of Section 1 and follows them through the correction of the paper. [Supplementary Note 5](technical-report-supplement.en.md#supplementary-note-5-formal-specification) states every definition precisely and argues the two propositions of Section 4.4. Appendix A sets the objects side by side and draws the lifecycle of a requirement, and Appendix B.1 gives each definition's implementation status.

### Keep: research state and admission

To resume the comparison weeks later, the researcher needs five things rather than the files themselves. They are the question being answered, the evidence used and how it fits together, the judgment accepted and its scope, the conditions still open, and the step planned next. In the example these are the choice between $A$ and $B$, the paper's two benchmark sets, the judgment $J$ for targets in view, the unknown under the re-appearance condition, and the experiment on it.

**Research state** is these five, reconstructed from the records, and its open conditions also record conflicts and the cases in which a judgment does not apply. Because research state is a reconstruction rather than a summary, its sources and status can be checked and corrected, and how much it holds depends on what the question needs, not on how much material exists.

Keep, update and hold describe what happens to research state when a source changes. A judgment whose grounds the change does not touch is **kept**, with its grounds and scope. A judgment whose grounds are touched also stays, but a **requirement**, a registered piece of maintenance work, marks it for recheck (Section 4.2). It is **updated** when the researcher admits a candidate that narrows or replaces it, pinned to the changed version of the source. In the example, then, $J$ is updated, not deleted: its ground on the first set is untouched, so the admitted candidate narrows it.

Admitting that candidate does not by itself close the recheck of $J$, because a requirement also carries **checks**, tests it must pass, and the recheck of $J$ must still pass its check (Section 4.3). These definitions do not compute which ground a change touches: keep and update are followed for each ground only through that recheck.

Hold has two places, and the definitions keep them apart. A scientific condition that the evidence cannot settle stays open in research state, as the re-appearance condition does throughout the example. A step of maintenance that cannot yet be decided is held with a question to the researcher; by these definitions the recheck of $J$ is held after the correction (Section 4.3).

Three parties act on research state (Figure 4). The environment, ReSearchOS in the figure, selects context from the history it may use, reconstructs the research state and presents it with any candidate; an AI model proposes an output and a change; and the researcher decides what happens next. Whichever party starts it, every change has the same shape: select inputs for a purpose, check that the change may start, apply it, and check the result. Following typed graph transformation [Graph Transformation in a Nutshell](https://www.cs.le.ac.uk/people/rh122/papers/2006/Hec06Nutshell.pdf), this report calls such a change an **application** of a rule, and the rule declares what it reads, what it must preserve and what it intends to produce.

The shape matters because different errors are repaired at different steps (Figure 5). A misread number in the paper calls for correcting that observation and what depends on it. A ranking for targets in view, given in answer to a question about re-appearance, calls for clarifying the purpose first. Only an omission that recurs across tasks is grounds to propose revising the rule itself, and later tasks must then check the revision.

![**Only the researcher's admission turns a candidate into research state.** An AI model or agent receives permitted context, and its output and proposed change return only as a candidate: here the student's AI assistant's proposal about re-appearance, which the researcher refuses. The lines at the foot give what each party does and may not do.](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-three-parties-en.png)

![**Every change to research state has the same shape, and different errors call for different repairs.** **a**, The steps of a change, with its receipt kept in the history the next decision boundary reads. **b**, The three errors of the text, each numbered by the step of **a** where its repair applies.](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-application-en.png)

A candidate enters research state only through **admission**, and no single party can take that route alone. Five conditions must hold together. The researcher accepts the change; a trusted host or an actual instruction within scope authorizes it, which the candidate's own text cannot do; the change has actually been applied; and its checks have passed. Finally, a **receipt**, the record of what was done and checked, is kept in the history that the next decision boundary reads (Section 4.2). A change that never ran, or one without a receipt, therefore counts as neither applied nor checked. The researcher's acceptance settles the operational decision, not whether the judgment is scientifically true.

### Update: what a source change touches

Weeks after the comparison the paper is corrected from version $v_1$ to $v_2$. Two things then have to be settled: which version of the paper is now current, and which work the change touches. The system settles both at **decision boundaries**, the moments at which it looks at what has arrived and decides what to do next; work goes on between them. Each boundary has a cutoff, and a record that arrives after it waits for the next boundary. In the prototype a change is seen at the next `ros` command (Section 2).

The current version is not simply the newest file. An object's **current version candidates** are its versions with no known successor among the records the task may use. Two versions neither of which is known to follow the other therefore stay side by side as unresolved branches, and the choice between them is left to the researcher (Figure 6a). The prototype identifies versions differently (Section 5.4). In the example the answer is plain: once the correction is seen, the paper's current version is $v_2$.

The work the correction touches is found through what each requirement records, and the recheck of $J$ shows how. Like every requirement, it has a stable identity and is registered for a purpose, which carries the scientific conditions of the work and what its review relies on. It also records the inputs it depends on: here the paper, together with the versions of the rule and purpose behind the recheck. A change **touches** a registered requirement when one of those inputs has a new current version since the previous boundary. The recheck of $J$ depends on the paper, so the correction touches it; the re-appearance experiment rests on the open re-appearance condition rather than on the paper, so it is not touched (Figure 6b).

Registered requirements are not the only way work is found: when work is first set up, or a scoped check is rerun, a scan also finds needs, missing dependencies included. Supplementary Note 5 gives the remaining rules, for comparing versions, late-arriving records and scans. Once touched, a requirement joins the **unresolved set** and leaves it only through recorded resolution or cancellation. Deferral, waiting and an unsupported report of completion do not remove it, so work that nobody examined survives to the next boundary.

Touching therefore marks what to inspect, not what to edit. It follows recorded dependencies only, so it cannot discover a dependency that was never recorded. Had the correction fixed only a typo, the recheck of $J$ would still be touched with nothing in $J$ to change; conversely, a new study can make $J$ unreliable without changing any input $J$ depends on. A changed file is a reason to look, not a verdict.

### Hold: three-valued checks, held and waiting work

After the correction, can the recheck of $J$ be closed? That depends on whether the result on the first set still supports $J$, and only the researcher can judge this. The system therefore gives each check one of three results: pass, fail, or cannot yet tell, written 1, 0 and ?. A check passes or fails only when a qualified check that is currently valid establishes it; it cannot yet tell when the evidence is missing, stale or in unresolved conflict, and a ? never counts as a pass. A requirement is **resolved** only when all its checks pass on the same current receipts. This is why the old receipt for $v_1$ cannot close the recheck of $J$: it cannot answer for $v_2$.

Sometimes every check that has not passed cannot yet tell, and only the researcher can settle each of them. The system then asks the researcher one question for each, with a fixed set of options and only once for each version of the check's basis. The work stays there until the answers come, and a check that fails rules out holding. This is what **held** means. For the recheck of $J$, an answer that $J$ is still supported closes that check, and an answer that it is no longer supported reopens the recheck. Without an answer the recheck stays held and is never counted as done.

Asking once per basis version, and the prototype's `ros today` gathering the open questions into one list, are meant to keep the load of review down and keep review from becoming a habit (Section 3). Neither has been tested, and even a correction that only fixes a typo opens a recheck.

Work can also be **waiting** rather than held: it waits on a trigger that will bring it back, because the researcher deferred or narrowed it or because something outside the goal's scope blocks it. Deferring the re-appearance experiment to its scheduled date would make it waiting. Figure 7 shows what each combination of checks allows, Figure 18 in Appendix A draws the whole lifecycle of a requirement, and Supplementary Note 5 states the other limits on checks, holding and waiting.

Held and waiting work stays in view but neither resolves nor cancels a requirement. Only resolution or cancellation removes it from the unresolved set, and cancellation is a separate recorded decision to stop pursuing the need, which does not count as a repair. Even that closure is not permanent: a later change to the paper can touch the same requirement again.

(cannot yet tell) and their result.](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-hold-en.png)

### Composition and bounded goals

With the recheck of $J$ held and the experiment still ahead, two questions remain: when one step may build on the result of another, and when the work is done. The example answers the first. Success while the target stays in view may motivate the re-appearance experiment, but it may not be used to recommend $A$ once the target leaves the view. In general, one application may build on the result of another only when two sets of conditions are both established for the purpose: the conditions the first result carries, and those the second requires of its input.

A condition is established when the same current receipts pass every check the purpose declares for it, the same test as resolution. In the example the result is established only for targets in view, so it can motivate the experiment but cannot support recommending $A$ after re-appearance (Figure 8). An unchecked condition is not false, but it cannot authorize the next step.

![**A successor builds only on conditions established for its purpose.** **a**, What $J$ allows: success in view may motivate the experiment; recommending $A$ after the target leaves the view is not ready. **b**, What establishes a condition. An unknown is not satisfied, receipts for $v_1$ cannot answer for $v_2$, and compatibility of format is not a scientific premise.](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-composition-en.png)

The second question, when the work around the recheck of $J$ is finished, is harder than it sounds. In the early prototype, issues kept coming back after a cleanup was declared finished, across ten cleanup rounds and nearly two hundred commits. The definitions therefore confine the work to a **bounded goal**, a unit of work with a frozen scope, a frozen specification and an explicit outcome (Figure 9). The specification freezes the question, the purpose, the scope, the versions of rules and checks, what must be preserved, the authority, the budget, and the source versions with their cutoff, and it is admitted like any other candidate. Completion is then judged against something that cannot move during the run.

The scope is what the goal may read. Ownership is a tree that gives each record one owner, following where the record lives, while dependency can link any records. The goal covers the unclosed records of its project's node and the nodes below it, plus a few inputs from other projects read through the interfaces those projects declare, frozen with the specification. A read that bypasses an interface lies outside the frozen scope (Supplementary Note 5).

Within a bounded goal, being done and stopping are different things. The goal is **Done** when everything still open in it is either held on a question or waiting on a trigger, and none of the three non-exempt kinds of work remains open. These are in-scope work the run never evaluated, an earlier specification that cannot be reconstructed, and a confirmation still owed for a direct edit that bypassed admission (Supplementary Note 5). Those three may be held or waiting for a while, but they must close before the goal is Done, so a goal cannot end by accepting its own conclusion by default.

The goal stops when it is Done, when a part of its budget runs out, or when the researcher decides to stop, and what it reports depends on how it stopped. Done with nothing unresolved is complete. A goal can also be Done with held or waiting work still open, as in Figure 9b, and then it is complete_with_held. It lists that work with its questions and triggers, and reports the share of the goal's registered requirements that are resolved; a zero share is reported as deferred, not as completion. Otherwise, a goal stopped by its budget ends budget_stopped, and one stopped by the researcher ends human_stopped; neither reports completion.

![**A bounded goal freezes what it may read and must answer, and reports held or waiting work as open.** **a**, Scope: ownership (gray) and dependency (arrows). **b**, A general run to the first Stop: one requirement resolved, one held, one waiting; the last two stay open. **c**, The outcome, complete_with_held; closed with a receipt, the goal is kept as evidence.](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-three-axes-en.png)

Two propositions state what these definitions guarantee, and Supplementary Note 5 gives their hypotheses and arguments. The first says that goals whose frozen scopes do not overlap share no requirement, so neither's completion depends on the other. A goal on an unrelated project, for instance, neither adds to nor removes from the requirements of the goal that rechecks $J$. The second says that a goal either reaches Done or exhausts its budget in finitely many steps; besides its hypotheses it needs a condition on scheduling, because reopening a requirement, or an answer of “unsatisfied”, adds open work.

Both hold only under these conditions, and a finite budget of decision boundaries guarantees that a goal stops, not that it completes. Neither proposition shows that completion can be reached: that would need every unresolved requirement to be decidable within scope and budget, with no new source change.

## 5. The prototype: how it is built and what it does not yet do

This section shows how the prototype that ran the session of Section 2 is built and how far it stands from the definitions of Section 4. Part of keep, update and hold, and part of the researcher's decision among them, now run as commands, and the description holds at the two commits listed in Appendix B.3, where their tests are summarized.

### Responsibilities and the stable kernel

Section 3.3 requires that AI models, clients and plugins can be replaced while the researcher's sources stay authoritative. Figure 10 maps the target division drawn to meet it, in five rows: the clients, the methods, the stable kernel, the adapters and the records. The researcher owns the sources. The stable kernel computes changes and unresolved requirements from snapshots that adapters provide and never reads a source directly. Adapters handle files, execution and receipts through existing services without creating a second store of facts, and in that division they write only staged edits with receipts. Clients organize presentation, and model output can propose but cannot grant write authority, so it enters only as a candidate.

In the prototype, the session kernel that runs the commands of Section 2 is `ros.py`; behind it, the contracts of governance (what may become research state) and of coordination (what needs attention now) share one package. Admission itself is still carried out by `ros.py` rather than by that package, so the figure's single stable kernel is a target, not existing code (gap 3). Among the clients, agent sessions include Codex, Claude Code and others, and the configuration is `config/ros.json`. [Supplementary Note 2](technical-report-supplement.en.md#supplementary-note-2-session-operations-and-implementation-mappings) holds the commands, the record tables and the stepwise records behind this section.

![**Every dependency points toward a stable kernel that never reads a source directly; the figure draws the target division of responsibilities.** Clients and methods depend downward on the kernel, adapters upward. The wine-colored tags mark where the prototype differs: the Dashboard (gap 2), and `ros.py`, which still carries out admission itself (gap 3).](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-target-architecture-en.png)

Four structures sit behind the kernel: the record graph, that is, the records and the links between them; the rules; the unresolved requirements; and the execution traces. They are logical structures, not four databases: Markdown files and explicit decisions remain the researcher's sources, the state of a requirement is derived from the traces that already exist, and caches only speed up recovery. A trace establishes what was decided, attempted, observed and checked, but it cannot establish a dependency that was never recorded, and it has no authority over the researcher's sources. The prototype has further internal terms, and Supplementary Note 2 gives each with the contract it carries; a new category of term is justified only by a distinct contract or lifecycle. These boundaries are commitments of the design, and whether an implementation keeps to them has to be checked in that implementation.

### Session protocol and admission

Facts and judgments take different routes to research state (Figure 11), which is why the narrowed judgment of Section 2 went through admission instead of being written in directly. A fact may be updated within existing task authority, while a judgment passes through candidate, decision, actual change, check and receipt, each recorded separately. When `ros collect` or `ros propose` records the candidate, every basis it rests on is frozen at its full source version. If a basis changes during review, admission rejects the stale candidate and requires a new proposal, and an existing judgment can rebind its basis only through an explicit reconfirmation.

![**Facts and judgments take different routes to research state, and of the three channels that write a card, only the terminal records the researcher's decision.** **a**, The two routes, with candidate 1 of Section 2 as the judgment. **b**, The three channels and how each is recorded (gaps 1, 2).](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-admission-en.png)

Writeback must also land on the content it was planned against. If that content has changed by the time the write lands, the write is refused, the intervening edits are kept and no success receipt is issued. This is the refusal described in Section 2, and Supplementary Note 2 gives the mechanism and the paths it covers. These checks protect the built-in writeback paths, though they do not verify what an arbitrary rule must preserve (gap 9).

![**The downloadable version reconstructs an item's research state for the researcher but cannot save it.** The Research desk, opened on the fictional item Evaluation Pilot Decision; captured on 29 October 2026 from a fresh clone of the released downloadable version at commit f541cb6, not retouched. The right panel holds the progress record, the judgments, boundaries and source, and the next step with its acceptance criterion.](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-dashboard-en.png)

What an admission records depends on the channel it comes through. A judgment admission confirmed at the terminal is recorded as the researcher's decision. One executed by an agent is recorded as asserted by the invoker and still writes, so for an agent with shell access the record is attribution and an audit trail rather than a block. The terminal is recognized by an interactive prompt, which a process that allocates a pseudo-terminal can also answer, and that answer is then recorded as the researcher's decision: the record attests the channel, not the person (gap 1).

The Dashboard, the prototype's graphical client, makes lifecycle writes that bypass admission. When they are made during a session, `ros close` registers the fields that carry a judgment (state and priority) as unconfirmed changes that stay open until the researcher confirms them (gap 2). Figure 11b draws the three channels.

Closing a requirement is stricter. `ros decide … cancel/confirm` and `ros answer … cancel/satisfied` are accepted only at the terminal; an agent's relay is recorded as asserted by the invoker, and the requirement stays open. The same rule covers confirming, after the fact, a block written without admission: `ros admit --confirm` run by an agent is recorded as asserted by the invoker, and the confirmation requirement stays open. Closure also avoids asking twice. Fields carrying a judgment that a complete decision and a successful receipt already cover in the same session are not put to the researcher again. A later direct edit or an incomplete chain of changes still requires confirmation. One admission therefore neither asks the researcher to repeat a decision just made nor licenses arbitrary later changes.

### Clients, methods and trying it

The command line, where these decisions are taken, is the client that explicitly consumes the kernel's results. The Dashboard's cards, selections and calendars are projections, that is, views of the records, so they establish no scientific relation and count as no acceptance. Methods are in a similar position: they declare their inputs, outputs and checks, but declaring them gives a method no authority to accept anything. A rule of a kind that already exists is one JSON file. A new kind of executable check needs a change to the kernel and an architecture review, and until then the work it leaves unevaluated blocks completion (gap 12).

Whether each client keeps to these boundaries is checked client by client. Regression tests run without a graphical interface show neither that graphical interactions pass acceptance nor that external agents are under control, and those agents' hosts keep their own authority. The author's prototype and the downloadable version share the same scientific meaning and differ in authority; a packet that is copied carries no permission with it.

For the Dashboard's home page, the design first considered making the question, grounds, open conditions and next step the main entrance. The author preferred a calendar and resource cards for everyday use, so the home page keeps that familiar entrance and shows research state in a selected item's details. Selecting a resource for a handoff only assembles temporary context, whereas recording that it supports a conclusion is an assertion to be admitted, so the two should not share one drag action. Which arrangement other researchers would find better is still open; neither the author's preference nor the implementation checks can show it.

**Trying it.** The downloadable version is copy-only by default. Its shipped `config/ros.json` sets `runtime_scope: public_starter`, and the behavior below holds under that setting. A first session on its fictional fixture `examples/scheduler-demo` takes the steps of Section 2 with the commands in the right-hand column of Table 2. The downloadable version dispatches to no AI model provider and admits nothing into the researcher's sources, the records that count as authoritative, so a user can try recovery, context delivery and candidate collection, but not admission.

The two screenshots show how it presents an item, not task completion, accepted judgment or usability.

### What the prototype does not yet do

The session of Section 2 differs from the definitions of Section 4 in one place, and the difference runs through the whole session. By the definitions, the recheck of $J$ is held on a question to the researcher (Section 4.3 and Figure 7); in the session it stays open. The reason lies in what the prototype can check. The only check on the recheck that it can decide by itself is whether the basis is pinned to the current version, and after the correction that check fails (returns 0). A failed check is different from one that cannot yet tell, so there is nothing to hold and the recheck simply stays open. Whether the first-set result still supports $J$ is left to the researcher (gap 34, semantic checks).

The prototype holds a requirement only when it meets a check it cannot decide and must put to the researcher: `ros today` asks once, and the requirement stays held until the researcher answers with `ros answer`. No rule shipped with the prototype declares such a check, one that rests on the researcher's judgment. The held path is therefore reached in the session kernel's regression tests and otherwise only when a requirement's subject or basis no longer resolves (Appendix B.1).

This difference is one case of the general relation between the definitions and the code. Section 4, made precise in Supplementary Note 5, is the specification; the current code supplies selected executable paths, and coverage is recorded per obligation rather than inferred from test counts. Table 4 groups the most important gaps by theme, and [Supplementary Note 6](technical-report-supplement.en.md#supplementary-note-6-implementation-gaps-in-full) gives all 39 in full, under the numbers used throughout this report. The gaps bound what the implementation can claim. They leave the definitions of Section 4 and their conditional arguments unchanged, and the architecture of Section 5.1 assigns responsibilities rather than claiming that every composition or client exists.

Three gaps matter most to a user of the prototype. The first is that only the terminal path enforces the rule of authority: Dashboard writes and agent-executed admissions are recorded, as Section 5.2 describes, but not blocked (gaps 1 and 2). The second is that a source change is seen only at the next `ros` command, because no file watcher exists, and it does not propagate across several hops (gap 34). The third concerns waiting: a deferral the researcher confirms at the terminal with `ros decide … defer` does produce waiting (Appendix B.2), but no producer exists for waiting on an out-of-scope dependency (gap 17).

The prototype departs from the definitions in several further places. Besides leaving to the candidate which part a correction touches (Section 2, gap 11), it cannot replace or withdraw $J$; the researcher edits or removes it by hand. By the definitions, the unknown under the re-appearance condition stays among the open conditions and the experiment remains an unresolved requirement. The prototype has no rule to register a stated condition or a planned experiment, so `ros close` lists neither (gap 12). The experiment would be waiting only if the researcher deferred it at the terminal with `ros decide … defer`. The session makes no such deferral, so the experiment stays unresolved and the goal is not Done.

The prototype also identifies a version by its content digest alone, so a return to earlier content counts as the earlier version rather than as a new revision (Appendix B.1). Finally, it does not implement complete conditional composition (gap 10), sessions only approximate bounded goals (gap 15), and cross-client integration remains unverified.

Two kinds of evidence stay separate. The regression tests of the kernel and of writeback exercise source changes, proposal freezing, admission, refusal of concurrent writes and closure records on temporary fixtures, and passing them establishes those execution properties for those inputs. Whether the judgments for the in-view and re-appearance conditions are right, whether the experiment is worth running and whether the researcher understood more are questions for actual tasks and independent evidence.

## 6. Neighboring systems, the strongest counter-case and conclusion

Closing these gaps would leave two questions: whether the tools researchers already use already do what ReSearchOS sets out to do, and whether a strong model with a good handoff would be enough. Setting ReSearchOS beside neighboring systems answers the first; only the comparison described in Section 6.2 can answer the second.

### Neighboring systems

The nearest alternatives are the tools researchers already use, not research agents, so the comparison corrects the paper of the running example in each class of tool. Table 5 records, class by class, what is kept, what is updated when a source changes and what can be left open; a row describes the class as its named or typical systems report it, not every system in it. Figure 14 shows how the correction plays out in six of the classes. None of the six holds the change open as a question for the researcher, as the last column of Table 5 also records.

In a note tool the corrected paper changes nothing: the note that records $J$ stays as it was until the researcher edits it, by hand or through an assistant. Version control keeps every version of the paper's record, merges a concurrent branch and refuses a stale write. It refuses by optimistic concurrency control [Optimistic concurrency control](https://doi.org/10.1145/319566.319567), also the mechanism behind the prototype's refusal of a changed file, and it detects concurrent branches as version vectors do [Version vectors](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.1983.236733). Version control does not, however, distinguish a fact from a judgment, and its answer to a conflict is to merge. ReSearchOS instead keeps conflicting branches as current version candidates ([Supplementary Note 5](technical-report-supplement.en.md#supplementary-note-5-formal-specification)) and leaves the choice to the researcher.

Several classes resolve the correction themselves. In a truth maintenance system, with the two benchmark sets drawn as separate justifications, the one through the second set fails and $J$ stays in through the first (Figure 14c). Such a system [Truth maintenance system](https://doi.org/10.1016/0004-3702%2879%2990008-0) records why each belief stands and revises beliefs along those recorded justifications. Once the reason for retracting an assumption fails, the assumption is restored, and a belief that still has another valid justification stays in. The logic-based TMS [Logic-based TMS](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/1990/AAAI90-164.pdf) labels each belief true, false or unknown, and the assumption-based TMS [ATMS](https://doi.org/10.1016/0004-3702%2886%2990080-9) keeps every consistent context at once, without retraction.

The checks of Section 4.3 use the same three values, and the touched set of Section 4.2 is simpler, marking requirements only one step along recorded dependencies. What differs is who gives the value. In a truth maintenance system the system relabels, and unknown means that propagation derived neither true nor false. In ReSearchOS, a check that the system cannot decide takes its value from the researcher, within a frozen scope, so a change can leave the recheck of a judgment held for the researcher. Belief revision in the AGM tradition [AGM](https://doi.org/10.2307/2274239) defines how a theory changes through functions governed by a set of postulates, whereas keep, update and hold name what happens to the researcher's records and are not revision operators.

A build system rebuilds the result with the person outside the loop. Build systems [Build systems à la carte](https://doi.org/10.1145/3236774) decide what a changed input invalidates, and those with early cutoff stop when a rebuilt value is unchanged, which is the update operation for artifacts. A suspended build task, however, waits for its dependencies, not for a person's judgment.

Agent memory keeps and retrieves, and when the paper changes it overwrites or invalidates the old chunk as the agent decides. Graphiti [Zep](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13956) invalidates a contradicted fact instead of deleting it, GitHub Copilot Memory [Copilot Memory](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/building-an-agentic-memory-system-for-github-copilot/) checks the code locations a memory cites against the current branch before use, and PACMI [PACMI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05732) marks the memories that depend on changed evidence as needing verification. In all three, the system sets the new status and no person is asked. GovMem [GovMem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02579) routes a doubtful candidate memory to human review, but only before it is written, and describes no later recheck of a memory once it is promoted.

An AI-scientist system reruns, and keeps, updates and produces artifacts, under its own authority. Agent Laboratory [Agent Laboratory](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.320/) accepts a researcher's feedback at each stage, and Kosmos [Kosmos](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02824) cites every statement of its reports to code or literature within a run of up to twelve hours. Neither these systems nor the build systems and agent memory above record an admitted, scoped judgment that a later source change reopens.

Research knowledge graphs and experiment trackers update the record of the paper, not the grounds of a judgment built on it. A research knowledge graph [Open Research Knowledge Graph](https://doi.org/10.1145/3360901.3364435) would make the paper and its comparison a first-class object, with versions and provenance for the stored data; the judgment protocol here adds the scope, checks and authority that decide whether a judgment built on it still holds. Experiment trackers and provenance stores keep runs and lineage; they answer what was run, not whether a judgment still holds.

Other tools bring the change back to a person, a practice older than this report. Requirements tools such as IBM Engineering Lifecycle Management mark a trace link suspect when the content of either linked artifact changes, and a team member then marks it valid or invalid [Link validity](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/engineering-lifecycle-management-suite/lifecycle-management/7.0.3?topic=management-link-validity). On GitHub, a protected branch can be set to dismiss an approval once new commits change the diff it approved, and the change cannot merge until someone approves it again [Protected branches](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-protected-branches/about-protected-branches).

Living systematic reviews bring research conclusions back to their authors in a similar way, usually when new evidence is found. The authors judge whether new studies are likely to have an important impact on the review's findings, and readers are told that an update is pending while the new evidence is integrated [Cochrane living systematic reviews](https://resources.cochrane.org/sites/resources.cochrane.org/files/uploads/inline-files/Transform/201912_LSR_Revised_Guidance.pdf). In June 2026 Cochrane announced that it matches retracted studies to the reviews that include them, and that it will contact the authors, add editorial notes to affected reviews while the impact is assessed, and leave the decision on the conclusions to its editors [Cochrane retracted publications](https://www.cochrane.org/about-us/news/cochrane-strengthens-systems-manage-retracted-publications-its-published-reviews).

Closer to a single claim, a ledger of the quantitative claims in one paper [Provable discourse](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10450) fingerprints the data behind each claim. It flags a claim as stale when that data changes even if the claim still passes, and it blocks the claims that depend on one that fails.

One recent system carries a retraction down a whole chain of records. In a wiki maintained by a language model for regulated settings, Zhang's provenance layer [Provenance-native wiki](https://doi.org/10.53941/aiet.2026.100010) binds claims to evidence passages. When a source is marked retracted, superseded or downgraded, it follows those links to the dependent claims, rules and decisions. A claim with other support has its confidence recomputed and is marked disputed below a threshold, a claim with none is suspended, and the decisions downstream are flagged; a decision whose confidence falls below a floor is flagged for human review. Five simulated retractions on a 112-document corpus reached every affected record. This is the nearest mechanism we found to the touched set of Section 4.2, and it reports a controlled evaluation, which ReSearchOS does not. The difference lies in who acts and on what: the system sets the status of a whole claim by threshold, and the paper describes no scope on a claim and no question to a person about which part still stands.

What these hold open is a link, a pending change, a whole review or a whole claim. None keeps admitted research judgments with their scope and basis version, updates only the part a change touches through a candidate the researcher admits, and holds a check with a question for the researcher. The comparison also cuts the other way: a new study can make a judgment unreliable without changing any input the judgment depends on (Section 4.2). Living reviews are built for that case, and ReSearchOS does not notice it. The ReSearchOS row of Table 5 describes the design mechanisms, and Section 5.4 states how much of them is executable.

| Agent memory (RAG, A-MEM, Mem0, MemOS, Graphiti, PACMI) | Retrieved and compressed context | Overwrites, re-ranks, or invalidates as the agent decides | At most a needs-verification mark set by the system; nothing put to the researcher |
| AI-scientist systems (AI Scientist, Agent Laboratory, Co-Scientist, Kosmos, Denario) | Hypotheses, experiments and artifacts | Reruns under their own authority; a researcher may give feedback on a stage | Nothing the researcher decides |
| ReSearchOS | Judgments with scope and basis version | Only the touched part, through a candidate the researcher admits | Open scientific conditions, and checks with a question for the researcher |

Researcher authority itself has precedents, and what matters in each is what happens to an approval when the paper is corrected. OmniScientist's OSP protocol [OmniScientist](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.16931) records a human's approval of an artifact, with its version hash, in a provenance chain kept on a central hub. What a human approves there is an artifact that an agent submits for review, not a scoped judgment that a later source change reopens. In the running example, approving only the ranking of $A$ over $B$ and passing it to the next task would conceal the untested re-appearance condition.

Two recent systems come closer on researcher authority. Magnolia [Magnolia](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27230) applies proposed cross-project rules only after a researcher confirms each one, and ResearchLoop [ResearchLoop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28282) admits a claim only within the scope that a gate, written by the researcher or a prespecified protocol file, approves. Neither paper, however, describes returning an admitted result to the researcher when one of its sources later changes. In Magnolia, a rule tied to a superseded tool version stays until the researcher revises or retires it; in ResearchLoop, the recheck is a deterministic gate evaluation that downgrades or blocks a claim whose evidence chain is missing or mismatched.

Figure 15 places most classes of Table 5, and several systems named in this section, on two axes. One is what a source change leaves open, taken from the last column of Table 5 or from the paragraph that describes the system. The other is who acts on the change: the system or an agent, which relabels, merges, rebuilds, reruns or overwrites; a person, who edits, re-approves or updates the whole item directly; or the researcher, who admits a candidate for the touched part of a judgment. Research knowledge graphs and experiment trackers are not placed, because Table 5 does not say who updates their record. Only ReSearchOS holds the touched part open and resolves it through admission.

The comparisons above record mechanisms as their sources report them, not integration or performance; [Supplementary Note 4](technical-report-supplement.en.md#supplementary-note-4-research-foundations-in-detail) gives the full synthesis, including the retrieval, memory and AI-scientist systems behind Table 5 and other work that supplies parts of the design. One point from Section 3.4 also carries over: accumulation must accommodate correction and must not be confused with human learning. Studies of generative AI and learning [GenAI and human learning](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02004-5), of human–AI combinations [Human–AI combinations](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02024-1) and of feedback loops [Human–AI feedback loops](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02077-2) therefore motivate evaluating three things separately: artifacts, strategy reuse and the researcher's learning. Whether the environment is needed at all lies beyond these comparisons, and that is the question of the counter-case.

### The strongest counter-case and the smallest form

![**One comparison would make most of this report unnecessary, and the evidence so far decides nothing.** **a**, The comparison, not yet run, so no cell is scored. **b**, The smallest form (blue frame) if the handoff wins, and the four items that could then be retired. **c**, The evidence so far on each side.](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-counter-case-en.png)

One result would make most of this report unnecessary, and the design documents behind Section 3 name the comparison that could produce it: a strong file-aware model given a one-page versioned handoff, set against ReSearchOS over enough natural research tasks with equal sources, tools, models and budgets. The handoff wins if the model recovers the current question, the key grounds, the accepted judgments, the open boundaries and the next action within declared quality and critical-error limits, at lower total effort, and in two consecutive comparisons. If the handoff wins, ReSearchOS should shrink to versioned research state, minimal context routing, researcher authority, provenance, and safe writeback with rollback. The Dashboard, low-value plugins, complex state and the separate operating-system narrative could then be retired. Shrinking is a success the principles permit, not a failure (Figure 16).

The evidence so far points both ways and decides nothing. On one side are four requests from the author's reconstructed ninety-day design history, listed in Table 6 and drawn in Figure 17 in the style of Figure 1. In each, a resumed thread is known to have lacked a condition or a destination that the record of the previous session did not carry. They are the only such cases so far, and what was missing in them is what a one-page handoff would have to supply. Since none of them is a source change, this report suggests that the natural tasks of the comparison include one in which a source changes, as the paper does in the running example.

### Conclusion

ReSearchOS treats continuity in research as a question about the research state carried forward rather than about files. It answers the question in three parts that constrain each other: principles say what must survive a change, definitions say what research state is and what the three operations on it require, and a reference architecture and prototype assign those operations to records, a kernel and clients. In the worked session, the prototype recovered the comparison, marked the judgment for recheck when its source changed and admitted the narrowed judgment only on the researcher's decision; it also recorded the researcher's refusal of the unsupported proposal and kept the recheck open at closure. These are starting paths, checkable at the terminal.

Long-term benefit has not been shown. Neither of the two studies of Section 6.2 has been run, and the results of each should revise the definitions or the prototype before any value is claimed. Where a clear handoff is sufficient, the simpler arrangement should be preferred; additional structure deserves to remain only where it helps researchers notice changed conditions, revise judgments and avoid repeated work. This returns to the answer the report began with: what has to be kept is research state, not research files, and when a source changes, the researcher, not the AI model or another session, decides which part is kept, updated or left open.

Of these objects it is the requirement that carries the maintenance work, and Figure 18 draws its lifecycle. A requirement is touched when one of its recorded inputs changes, compared by revision digest, or when a scan finds it as work is set up or a scoped check is rerun. It closes only when every check is 1 on one bundle of receipts bound to current versions, or when a recorded decision with a reason cancels it. Until then, an answer of 0 or the trigger returns held work to plain unresolved work, and the trigger reopens waiting work.

![**A requirement stays unresolved until it is closed on the record; held and waiting work is still unresolved.** **a**, How a requirement enters, waits and closes. **b**, The three check values; a check never evaluated has none. **c**, When a bounded goal is Done, and the outcome read off at the first Stop.](figures/architecture/researchos-r9-lifecycle-en.png)

Holding and waiting keep a requirement in view without closing it, since only resolution and cancellation remove it from the unresolved set. A 0 is established non-satisfaction and keeps the work unresolved; a check never evaluated has no value and counts as unevaluated work. A bounded goal is Done when every unresolved registered requirement is held or waiting and no unevaluated work, unreconstructable specification or owed confirmation remains unresolved. A goal with held or waiting work can therefore still be Done, as complete_with_held, or as deferred if none of its requirements is resolved.

### Sessions as approximate bounded goals

The prototype has no separate goal object: a session stands in for the bounded goal of Section 4.4. The command `ros today` opens the session and freezes its specification, which covers the purpose and its version, the target with its scope and digest, the rules and fields to preserve, the authority, the budget, and the sources with their cutoff. Running it again on the same day for the same target keeps that snapshot and reports what differs, and an admitted revision affects only the next goal. With `--for`, the session narrows to one commitment, its judgments, the cards that hold them, its source card and its basis cards. A target that does not exist or is ambiguous opens no session, and an empty or absent scope snapshot cannot report complete or complete_with_held (gap 16).

The budget covers boundaries, questions and reopenings and is frozen with the specification. Each boundary is charged to its session, including one at which only waiting continues, so the budget counts boundaries rather than elapsed time. Exhaustion stops questions, dispatch and further boundaries, but not the maintenance of requirements or the recording of reopenings, and a session then closed unfinished is budget_stopped unless old held questions exempt it. Gaps 19–22 record where metering and accounting are incomplete.

Waiting is exempt from completion only when the researcher put it there with a trigger that has not expired: by a triggered deferral or narrowing through the researcher's own channel, or by confirming `ros decide … defer` at the terminal. A deferral confirmed at the terminal therefore does produce waiting, while waiting on an out-of-scope dependency has no producer (gap 17). A deferral asserted off the terminal (`invoker_asserted`), an unconfirmed legacy deferral, an executor's deferral, a dependency inside the scope, and waiting without a trigger or past it grant no exemption.

Some things always block completion: an unreconstructable earlier specification, a break-glass block or field confirmation, in-scope unevaluated work, and a dispatched run without a usable result, including one that failed with unknown effects. A failure before dispatch does not. Answer-triggered holds can nonetheless rest on invalid old questions (gap 18). An explicit abandonment records the unknown effects without a receipt and lets the session settle.

At closure the session counts requirements, not inputs, and only in-scope unevaluated work affects completion. Early closure is recorded as `human_stopped`, all questions are listed regardless of scope, and an unverifiable receipt cannot exclude an existing in-scope requirement. Each boundary freezes enough of its inputs for a later boundary to tell a requirement missed under the earlier specification from one caused by a change. A cold start, or a missing earlier specification, is recorded as initial; in the second case the miss status is unknown, and one requirement for the unreconstructable specification is registered, or reopened if it was closed. Attribution of new requirements to scope, source and version changes is partial, and semantic caching is absent (gaps 23–25).
